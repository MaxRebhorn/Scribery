## Page 1 — So, You Want to Learn Grammar?

Welcome back!

If you've read my *[Greek Alphabet Primer](book:greek_alphabet_primer#contents)*, you should now be familiar with all twenty-four Greek letters. You can look at a Greek word without immediately assuming someone spilled a collection of strange symbols onto the page.

That's progress.

But knowing the letters is only the beginning. You might be able to read the word ANTHRŌPOS, for example, but do you know what it does in a sentence? Is it the subject? The object? Does it belong to someone? Is it just sitting there, looking important?

That's where grammar comes in.

Grammar is the set of rules that explains how words work together. It tells us how words change their forms, how they relate to one another, and how we can turn a pile of words into something another person might actually understand.

Greek grammar has a reputation for being complicated. And, to be fair, it has done quite a lot to earn that reputation.

The Greeks apparently looked at a perfectly good word and thought: "What if we gave it five different endings?"

Still, don't panic. We'll take things one step at a time.

This book covers the foundations of Koine Greek: nouns, genders, cases, articles, adjectives and verbs. It won't teach you every possible form. That would require a much larger book, and I suspect you've already spent enough emeralds.

Let's begin.

## Page 2 — Nouns: More Than Just Words

Let's start with nouns.

A **noun** is a word used to name a person, place, thing or idea.

Some examples in English:

- Man
- House
- Book
- Wisdom
- Water

Greek has nouns too. Shocking, I know.

Here are a few examples:

- ANTHRŌPOS — person, human being
- OIKOS — house, household
- BIBLOS — book, scroll
- SOPHIA — wisdom
- HUDŌR — water

Notice that Greek nouns don't always look the same when they appear in different sentences.

For example, the word ANTHRŌPOS can appear as ANTHRŌPOS, ANTHRŌPOU or ANTHRŌPON.

No, these aren't three different words.

They're different forms of the same noun. The ending changes depending on the word's role in the sentence.

This is one of the most important features of Greek grammar.

In English, we often use word order to show what a noun is doing. In Greek, the endings do a lot of that work.

Which is useful, I suppose. It also means there are quite a few endings to learn.

We'll get to those soon enough.

First, there's another little detail about Greek nouns.

They have genders.

Yes, even objects get assigned a gender. Don't ask me why. I wasn't there when they decided.

## Page 3 — The Three Genders

Greek nouns belong to one of three grammatical genders:

- **Masculine**
- **Feminine**
- **Neuter**

These are grammatical categories. They don't necessarily tell you anything about whether something is male, female or alive.

For example:

- O ANTHRŌPOS — the person
- HĒ OIKIA — the house, home
- TO DŌRON — the gift

A person is masculine in this example, a house is feminine, and a gift is neuter.

Does that mean every person is grammatically masculine? No. Does it mean houses are secretly female? Also no.

It's simply how the language classifies these nouns.

The important thing is that other words connected to a noun often have to agree with its gender.

This includes articles and adjectives.

For example:

- O KALOS ANTHRŌPOS — the good person
- HĒ KALĒ OIKIA — the good house
- TO KALON DŌRON — the good gift

Notice how KALOS changes its ending each time.

That's agreement. The adjective changes to match the noun.

We'll discuss adjectives in more detail later.

For now, remember this: when you learn a Greek noun, learn its gender too.

Don't just memorize ANTHRŌPOS and move on. Remember that it is masculine.

Your future self will thank you.

Or blame you, depending on how much you've forgotten.

## Page 4 — Cases: Why Does Every Word Have So Many Endings?

Now we arrive at one of the most important parts of Greek grammar.

Cases.

A **case** tells us what role a noun or pronoun plays in a sentence. Greek uses different endings to show these roles.

Koine Greek has five traditional cases:

- Nominative
- Genitive
- Dative
- Accusative
- Vocative

The first four do most of the work in ordinary sentences. The vocative is mainly used when addressing someone directly.

Let's take a closer look.

### 1. Nominative — The Naming Case

The word *nominative* comes from the Latin *nominare*, meaning "to name".

That's a useful clue. The nominative is commonly used for the subject of a sentence: the person or thing doing something, or the thing being described.

Example:

- O ANTHRŌPOS BLEPEI — The person sees.

Who is seeing?

The person. That's our subject, so ANTHRŌPOS is in the nominative.

Think of the nominative as the case that names the main character in the sentence.

And no, it doesn't mean the person has been nominated for an award. Although perhaps they deserve one.

### 2. Genitive — The Case of Belonging

The word *genitive* is related to the Latin *genus*, meaning kind, origin or family.

The genitive often expresses possession, origin or a relationship between things.

Example:

- O OIKOS TOU ANTHRŌPOU — The person's house.

The form ANTHRŌPOU tells us whose house it is.

You can often translate a genitive with the English word "of".

The house **of the person**.

A rather useful little case, especially if you own a house and would like people to know about it.

## Page 5 — Cases, Continued

### 3. Dative — The Case of Giving

Here's a helpful connection: *dative* comes from the Latin *dare*, meaning "to give".

That makes it easier to remember.

The dative often marks the person or thing something is given or directed to. It can also express other relationships, such as means or location, depending on the construction.

Example:

- DIDŌMI TŌ ANTHRŌPŌ — I give to the person.

The form TŌ ANTHRŌPŌ is in the dative.

Who receives the gift?

The person. That's a typical use of the dative.

Of course, the dative has other jobs too. It would be far too convenient if one case only did one thing.

### 4. Accusative — The Case That Sounds Like an Accusation

Now this one is easy to remember.

**Accusative** sounds a lot like **accuse**.

And there really is a historical connection: both words are related to the Latin *accusare*, meaning "to accuse".

But don't get carried away. The accusative case does not mean that someone is being accused of something.

Its most common job is to mark the direct object: the person or thing directly affected by the action of a verb.

Example:

- BLEPŌ TON ANTHRŌPON — I see the person.

Who is doing the seeing?

I am.

What am I seeing?

The person.

The person is the direct object, so ANTHRŌPON is in the accusative.

No crime has been committed. At least, not in this sentence.

### 5. Vocative — When You're Calling Someone

The vocative is used when directly addressing someone.

For example:

- PHILE! — Friend!

You're not describing the friend or doing something to the friend. You're simply talking to them.

Think of shouting "Hey, friend!" across a room.

That's the vocative.

It doesn't appear with the same frequency as the other cases, but it's still worth knowing.

And please don't shout at people just to practise your Greek. I won't be responsible for the consequences.

## Page 6 — The Definite Article: The Little Word That Changes Everything

In English, we have a very useful little word: **the**.

Greek has one too, but it changes its form depending on the gender, number and case of the noun it accompanies.

It's called the **definite article**.

The article is the grammatical term for a word like "the". The word *article* itself comes from Latin *articulus*, meaning a small joint or part.

A rather strange name for such a small word, if you ask me.

Here are some examples:

- O ANTHRŌPOS — the person
- HĒ OIKIA — the house
- TO DŌRON — the gift

The article changes with the noun:

- O — masculine
- HĒ — feminine
- TO — neuter

Those are the nominative singular forms.

But remember the cases?

Yes, unfortunately, the article changes with those too.

For example, look at the masculine article:

| Case | Article | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Nominative | O | the |
| Genitive | TOU | of the |
| Dative | TŌ | to/for the |
| Accusative | TON | the |

The article helps you recognize the role of the noun, even when the word order changes.

For example:

- O ANTHRŌPOS BLEPEI TON OIKON.
- The person sees the house.

The article O marks the subject, while TON marks the direct object.

This is why learning the article is so useful. It gives you clues about the sentence before you've even finished reading it.

A small word doing a surprisingly large amount of work.

Perhaps I should start charging it rent.

## Page 7 — Adjectives: Describing Things

An **adjective** is a word that describes a noun.

In English, we use adjectives such as:

- Good
- Small
- Old
- Beautiful
- Strange

Greek works in much the same way, except that adjectives usually have to agree with the noun they describe.

Remember the three genders?

Adjectives change their endings to match the gender, number and case of the noun.

For example, let's look at the adjective KALOS, meaning "good".

- KALOS ANTHRŌPOS — good person
- KALĒ OIKIA — good house
- KALON DŌRON — good gift

The adjective changes its ending depending on the noun.

This is called **agreement**.

The adjective agrees with the noun in gender, number and case.

And yes, that means you have to pay attention to all three.

I know. It sounds like a lot of work just to say that something is good.

But there's a reason for it. These endings help show which words belong together, even if they aren't standing right next to each other.

A useful feature, really.

Just don't tell the Greeks I said that.

## Page 8 — Verbs: The Things That Actually Happen

So far, we've mostly talked about nouns and the words that describe them.

Now let's talk about verbs.

A **verb** describes an action, an event or a state.

Examples in English:

- See
- Give
- Speak
- Be
- Write

Greek verbs do the same thing, but they also carry quite a lot of grammatical information.

A Greek verb form can tell you:

- Who is performing the action.
- Whether the subject is singular or plural.
- What voice the verb uses.
- What mood the verb uses.
- What tense or aspect is involved.

That's quite a lot for one word.

Let's start with person and number.

### Person and Number

Greek verbs change depending on who is doing something.

There are three grammatical persons:

- **First person:** I or we.
- **Second person:** You.
- **Third person:** He, she, it or they.

There are also two main numbers:

- **Singular:** One person or thing.
- **Plural:** More than one.

For example, the Greek verb BLEPŌ means "I see".

Here are a few forms:

- BLEPŌ — I see
- BLEPEIS — You see
- BLEPEI — He, she or it sees

Notice how the ending changes.

You don't need to repeat the subject every time, because the verb ending often tells you who is doing the action.

That's convenient.

One less word to memorize. Finally, some good news.

## Page 9 — Tense and Aspect: Greek Has Opinions About Time

Now we come to one of the trickier parts.

Greek verbs have different tense forms, but there's something important to understand: Greek tense is not only about when something happens. It also expresses **aspect** — how an action is viewed.

Let's keep this simple.

Here are some important forms you'll encounter in Koine Greek:

- **Present:** Often describes an ongoing or repeated action in the present.
- **Imperfect:** Often describes an ongoing or repeated action in the past.
- **Aorist:** Presents an action as a complete whole, without focusing on its internal progress.
- **Future:** Often describes an action that will happen.
- **Perfect:** Often describes a present state resulting from a previous action.
- **Pluperfect:** Often describes a past state resulting from an earlier action.

The aorist deserves special attention.

You may be tempted to think that it simply means "past tense". That is a common beginner's mistake.

In the indicative mood, the aorist usually refers to past time. But its main aspect is perfective: it presents an action as a whole.

The present and imperfect commonly use imperfective aspect, showing an action as ongoing or repeated.

The perfect commonly emphasizes a resulting state.

Why does Greek need so many ways to describe an action?

I have no idea. Ask a Greek grammarian. Preferably one who hasn't had coffee yet.

For now, remember this:

**Tense tells you about the form of the verb. Aspect tells you how the action is being presented.**

They're related, but they aren't quite the same thing.

And if that sounds confusing, don't worry. It takes practice.

## Page 10 — Voice and Mood: Who Does What, and How?

Greek verbs also have voice and mood.

Let's look at them separately.

### Voice

Voice tells us how the subject relates to the action.

The three main voices are:

- **Active:** The subject performs the action.
- **Middle:** The subject is involved in the action in a particular way, often with some connection to their own interests or involvement.
- **Passive:** The subject receives the action.

For example, in English:

- The person washes the clothes. — Active.
- The person washes themselves. — A middle-like idea.
- The clothes are washed. — Passive.

The middle voice is not always easy to translate directly into English. Its meaning depends on the verb and the context.

Greek also has verbs that are middle or passive in form but have an active meaning. These are sometimes called deponent verbs.

Yes, another special category.

I promise the Greeks weren't doing this just to annoy us.

### Mood

Mood tells us how the speaker presents an action.

Some important moods are:

- **Indicative:** Presents something as a statement or question.
- **Subjunctive:** Often expresses possibility, purpose or something expected.
- **Imperative:** Gives a command or instruction.
- **Optative:** Expresses wishes or possibilities; it is much less common in later Koine Greek.

A few simple examples in English:

- "He writes." — Indicative.
- "So that he may write." — Subjunctive.
- "Write!" — Imperative.
- "May he write!" — Optative-like expression.

You don't need to master every mood immediately.

Just remember that Greek verbs aren't only about actions and time. They also show how the speaker wants to present those actions.

Quite a lot of responsibility for a single word.

## Page 11 — Putting It Together: Simple Sentences

Now let's see how these things work together.

We'll start with a simple sentence:

**O ANTHRŌPOS BLEPEI TON OIKON.**

The person sees the house.

Let's break it down:

- O — the, masculine nominative article.
- ANTHRŌPOS — person, nominative.
- BLEPEI — sees, third-person singular verb.
- TON — the, masculine accusative article.
- OIKON — house, accusative.

The person is doing the seeing, so the person is the subject.

The house is being seen, so it is the direct object.

Now another example:

**O ANTHRŌPOS DIDŌSI TO DŌRON TŌ PHILŌ.**

The person gives the gift to the friend.

Look at the cases:

- O ANTHRŌPOS — the person, nominative.
- TO DŌRON — the gift, accusative.
- TŌ PHILŌ — to the friend, dative.

Three nouns, three different jobs.

And the verb DIDŌSI tells us what is happening: someone is giving something.

Now you can see why cases matter. Without them, it would be much harder to understand who is giving what to whom.

A little more complicated than English, perhaps.

But at least the word endings are doing some of the work for you.

## Page 12 — A Few Things Worth Remembering

Let's review what we've covered.

**Nouns** name people, places, things and ideas. Greek nouns have grammatical gender and change form depending on their role in a sentence.

**Gender** comes in three varieties: masculine, feminine and neuter. It affects the forms of articles and adjectives.

**Cases** show the role of a noun or pronoun:

- Nominative — commonly the subject.
- Genitive — often possession or relationship.
- Dative — often the recipient or means.
- Accusative — commonly the direct object.
- Vocative — direct address.

**Articles** are words like "the". In Greek, they change to agree with the noun.

**Adjectives** describe nouns and agree with them in gender, number and case.

**Verbs** describe actions, events and states. Their forms can tell you about person, number, tense, aspect, voice and mood.

That's the foundation.

There are many more details, exceptions and special constructions. Greek grammar has enough of those to keep several generations of scholars occupied.

But you don't need to learn everything at once.

Start with recognizing the cases. Learn the articles. Practise identifying verbs. Read short sentences and work out how the words fit together.

And if you get something wrong, don't worry.

The ancient Greeks made mistakes too.

Probably.
