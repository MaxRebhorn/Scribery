## Page 1 — A Few Words Before We Begin

This book is meant to be your first step into the Greek language. And where better to start than with the fundamentals? The alphabet, of course!

Now, you might be wondering why this book contains nothing but the alphabet, and not a single word about grammar or anything else remotely useful.

Well, you didn't exactly have emeralds to spare, did you?

A proper education doesn't come cheap, you know.

The Greek alphabet may look a little daunting at first, but it's actually quite simple. Mostly. There are just two or three peculiar little details that might give you a headache. Nothing too serious, though. Probably.

You might even recognize some of the Greek letters by name already. Perhaps from calculus.

I always hated that. Gives me the creeps. Uuuugh. Math.

Always behaving as if it's so special and important, with all its fancy symbols and complicated equations. Honestly, who needs all that?

Meanwhile, we're here doing the truly important stuff: learning an ancient language that's been around for millennia and is mostly useful for translating texts that have already been translated.

Now *that's* what I call a productive use of one's time.

The important stuff, you know?

Anyway, let's get started.

— John Koine

## Page 2 — Meet the Alphabet

The Greek alphabet consists of twenty-four letters. That's twenty-four little symbols you'll need to get familiar with before you can even pretend to know what you're doing.

Each letter has a name, an uppercase form and a lowercase form.

Don't worry, though. Uppercase and lowercase are simply two ways of writing the same letter. You don't have to learn the alphabet twice. I wouldn't do that to you. Not without charging extra, anyway.

Greek has seven vowel letters:

**Α, Ε, Η, Ι, Ο, Υ, Ω**

The remaining seventeen letters are consonants.

Some letters will look familiar. Others might remind you of something you've seen in a mathematics textbook. Try not to let that ruin your day.

The Greek used in many texts from the Hellenistic and Roman periods is often called *Koine Greek*. The word *koine* means "common". It was a widely shared form of Greek used across much of the Mediterranean world.

Common Greek. How lovely. Finally, something that isn't unnecessarily complicated.

Now, on to the letters!

## Page 3 — The First Six

### 1. Alpha — Α α {#alpha}
**Latin letter: A**

The first letter of the Greek alphabet. Rather straightforward, really.

Example:

- ἀρχή — ARCHĒ — beginning, origin

A good place to start, wouldn't you say?

### 2. Beta — Β β {#beta}
**Latin letter: B**

Beta. You've probably heard this one before. It even sounds like the name of a rather unsuccessful experiment.

Example:

- βίβλος — BIBLOS — book, scroll

Quite appropriate for a book, I suppose.

### 3. Gamma — Γ γ {#gamma}
**Latin letter: G**

Gamma is one of those letters that looks a little like a strange corner someone forgot to finish drawing.

Example:

- γλῶσσα — GLŌSSA — tongue, language

Remember this one. Language is rather important when learning a language.

### 4. Delta — Δ δ {#delta}
**Latin letter: D**

Another familiar one! Delta is particularly popular with mathematicians. They even borrowed its uppercase shape for their equations.

Honestly, can't they leave anything alone?

Example:

- δῶρον — DŌRON — gift

### 5. Epsilon — Ε ε {#epsilon}
**Latin letter: E**

Epsilon is a short vowel. Think of it as the simpler cousin of Eta, who apparently needed a little extra room.

Example:

- ἔργον — ERGON — work, deed

You'll notice that the little accent mark doesn't appear in the Latin version. We'll get to those marks later.

### 6. Zeta — Ζ ζ {#zeta}
**Latin letter: Z**

Zeta. A rather pleasant letter, if you ask me. Not particularly troublesome.

Example:

- ζωή — ZŌĒ — life

And that's the first six! See? You're practically an expert already.

Please don't put that on your résumé.

## Page 4 — The Next Six

### 7. Eta — Η η {#eta}
**Latin letter: Ē**

Now here's where things get slightly more interesting.

Eta is not the same as Epsilon. It represents a different vowel, and we show that difference with a line above the E: **Ē**.

Don't forget that little line. It isn't decoration. We're not making jewellery here.

Example:

- ἡμέρα — HĒMERA — day

Notice the H at the beginning? We'll explain that little fellow later.

### 8. Theta — Θ θ {#theta}
**Latin letters: TH**

Theta is a single Greek letter, even though we write it with two Latin letters: T and H.

Yes, one Greek letter, two Latin letters. Keep that in mind.

Example:

- θεός — THEOS — god

### 9. Iota — Ι ι {#iota}
**Latin letter: I**

A simple little letter. Nothing particularly complicated about this one.

You may have heard the expression "not one iota". It refers to something very small or insignificant.

Which is rather unfair to Iota, if you ask me.

Example:

- ἰχθύς — ICHTHUS — fish

Why fish? Why not. They're perfectly respectable creatures.

### 10. Kappa — Κ κ {#kappa}
**Latin letter: K**

Kappa looks quite familiar to anyone who knows the Latin alphabet.

Example:

- καρδία — KARDIA — heart

Not to be confused with the heart-shaped things people keep drawing on their letters. Completely different subject.

### 11. Lambda — Λ λ {#lambda}
**Latin letter: L**

Lambda has a rather simple shape. Its uppercase form looks a little like a roof.

I suppose that's useful if you're a house.

Example:

- λόγος — LOGOS — word, speech, reason

A rather important word in Greek, as it happens. But that's a story for another, more expensive book.

### 12. Mu — Μ μ {#mu}
**Latin letter: M**

Mu. Another letter that should feel quite familiar.

It's also the Greek letter mathematicians use for various things. I won't get into that. I've had enough trouble already.

Example:

- μέτρον — METRON — measure

And yes, that's where the word "metre" comes from. You're welcome.

## Page 5 — Getting Further Along

### 13. Nu — Ν ν {#nu}
**Latin letter: N**

Nu looks almost exactly like our Latin N, at least in its uppercase form.

The lowercase version is a little different, so don't mix it up with anything else.

Example:

- νόμος — NOMOS — law, custom

A lovely word. Especially when the laws are written by someone sensible.

### 14. Xi — Ξ ξ {#xi}
**Latin letter: X**

Ah, Xi. A particularly interesting one.

Its uppercase form looks like three horizontal lines stacked on top of one another. Perhaps someone was trying to draw a very small ladder.

Example:

- ξένος — XENOS — stranger, foreigner, guest

The letter is represented by X. And no, you don't need to write KS. We already have enough things to remember.

### 15. Omicron — Ο ο {#omicron}
**Latin letter: O**

Omicron means "little O". Its counterpart, Omega, means "great O".

The Greeks apparently felt that their vowels needed a size classification.

Example:

- οἶκος — OIKOS — house, household

Very useful if you happen to own one.

### 16. Pi — Π π {#pi}
**Latin letter: P**

Pi! Yes, that Pi.

Mathematicians seem rather fond of this one. Something about circles and numbers that never quite end.

I won't ask.

Example:

- πνεῦμα — PNEUMA — breath, wind, spirit

A perfectly good letter, despite its unfortunate mathematical reputation.

### 17. Rho — Ρ ρ {#rho}
**Latin letter: R**

Rho looks a little like P, which can be confusing if you're not paying attention.

Remember: uppercase Rho is Ρ, not the Latin P.

Some Greek words also place a special breathing mark above an initial Rho. When that happens, the Latin spelling begins with RH.

Example:

- ῥῆμα — RHĒMA — word, utterance

Yes, RH. Not a spelling mistake.

### 18. Sigma — Σ σ ς {#sigma}
**Latin letter: S**

Sigma gets two lowercase forms. Two!

Apparently one wasn't enough.

The form σ is used at the beginning or in the middle of a word, while ς appears at the end.

Both are exactly the same letter. They simply look different depending on their position.

Example:

- σοφία — SOPHIA — wisdom

We'll give Sigma its own little [section](#sigma-and-its-two-faces) later. It clearly needs the attention.

## Page 6 — The Final Six

### 19. Tau — Τ τ {#tau}
**Latin letter: T**

Tau is another straightforward letter.

Its uppercase form looks like a capital T. How convenient.

Example:

- τέκνον — TEKNON — child

Nothing particularly strange here. Let's enjoy the moment while it lasts.

### 20. Upsilon — Υ υ {#upsilon}
**Latin letter: U**

Now this is an interesting one.

Upsilon is represented by U in the system used throughout this book.

You might encounter other transliteration systems that use Y instead. Don't let that confuse you. Different systems make different choices.

Example:

- ὕδωρ — HUDŌR — water

And yes, the H is intentional. More on that shortly.

### 21. Phi — Φ φ {#phi}
**Latin letters: PH**

Phi is another letter represented by two Latin letters.

Just like Theta, this means two Latin letters for one Greek letter.

Example:

- φῶς — PHŌS — light

At this point, you may be noticing a pattern.

I'm afraid we're not finished.

### 22. Chi — Χ χ {#chi}
**Latin letters: CH**

Chi. Don't confuse it with Xi.

I know. They look similar, they sound similar in their English names, and the Greeks probably thought that was perfectly reasonable.

Example:

- χαρά — CHARA — joy

Remember: CH represents one Greek letter, not two.

### 23. Psi — Ψ ψ {#psi}
**Latin letters: PS**

Another letter represented by two Latin letters.

Perhaps the Greeks simply enjoyed making everyone else's lives harder. I wouldn't put it past them.

Example:

- ψυχή — PSUCHĒ — soul, life

A rather interesting word with several meanings. But we'll leave that to the people who can afford the lexicon.

### 24. Omega — Ω ω {#omega}
**Latin letter: Ō**

And finally, Omega!

Remember Omicron? This is its larger counterpart. The name means "great O".

We show it with a line above the O: **Ō**.

Example:

- ὥρα — HŌRA — hour, season

And that's it! All twenty-four letters.

Congratulations. You've reached the end of the alphabet without losing your mind.

A rare achievement, honestly.

## Page 7 — Sigma and Its Two Faces

Remember Sigma?

Well, here is the promised explanation.

Most Greek letters have one uppercase form and one lowercase form. Sigma is a little different because its lowercase form changes depending on where it appears in a word.

It has three written forms in total:

| Form | Name |
|---|---|
| Σ | Uppercase Sigma |
| σ | Lowercase Sigma |
| ς | Final Sigma |

The rule is quite simple:

- Use σ at the beginning and in the middle of a word.
- Use ς at the end of a word.

For example:

**σοφός**

The word begins with σ and ends with ς.

Both are Sigma. Nothing has changed except the appearance.

This is a writing convention. It does not represent two different letters.

And no, there isn't a secret fourth Sigma. I've checked.

## Page 8 — So, How Do We Write This in Latin Letters?

Now that we've met all twenty-four letters, there's one question left: how do we write Greek words using the letters we're more familiar with?

The process is called **transliteration**.

That's a rather long word for a fairly simple idea: we represent the letters of one writing system using letters from another.

In this case, we use Latin letters to write Greek words.

For example:

- λόγος becomes LOGOS
- θεός becomes THEOS
- ψυχή becomes PSUCHĒ

Notice that some Greek letters need two Latin letters:

- Θ becomes TH
- Φ becomes PH
- Χ becomes CH
- Ψ becomes PS

Each of these is still just one Greek letter. Don't go counting TH as two Greek letters. That would be a very silly mistake, and I'd hate for you to embarrass yourself.

A few Greek letters also need a little extra marking in our Latin spelling:

- Η becomes Ē
- Ω becomes Ō

That line above the E or O matters. It helps distinguish these letters from Epsilon and Omicron.

You may come across other books that use slightly different spellings. That's because transliteration isn't always done in exactly the same way. For the examples in this book, stick to the forms shown here.

And remember: transliteration is not translation. LOGOS tells you how λόγος is written in Latin letters. It doesn't tell you what the word means.

For that, I'm afraid you'll need another book.

Conveniently, I happen to know a fellow who writes those.

## Page 9 — What Are All Those Little Marks?

Greek doesn't always leave its letters alone.

You'll sometimes find small marks above them. These are called **diacritics**. They might look like somebody dropped a few crumbs onto the page, but they actually carry information.

Let's start with the breathing marks.

### Smooth and Rough Breathing

Words beginning with a vowel or diphthong usually carry one of two breathing marks. Initial Rho also has a breathing mark.

**Smooth breathing**

The smooth breathing is written like a tiny opening mark: ἀ.

It indicates that there is no H sound at the beginning of the word.

**Rough breathing**

The rough breathing looks like a tiny closing mark: ἁ.

It indicates an H sound at the beginning of the word.

For example:

- ἀρχή → ARCHĒ
- ἡμέρα → HĒMERA
- ὥρα → HŌRA
- ῥῆμα → RHĒMA

See? The rough breathing gives us that H. With an initial Rho, the H follows the R.

Now, what about the other marks?

### Accent Marks

Greek uses three main accent marks:

- **Acute:** ά
- **Grave:** ὰ
- **Circumflex:** ᾶ

These marks are part of Greek spelling and are useful when reading and studying the language. They are connected to the way words were pronounced in ancient Greek, although the details changed over time.

You may also encounter a **diaeresis**, as in ϊ, which shows that two vowels should be read separately, and the **iota subscript**, as in ᾳ.

So, are all these marks important?

Yes, absolutely.

Will we reproduce every single one when writing Greek words with Latin letters in this book?

No.

The breathing marks are the exception: rough breathing is represented by H. The accents, diaeresis and iota subscript are left out of the simplified spellings used here.

Why?

Well, adding another few pages to explain every little mark would have made this book more expensive. And judging by the number of emeralds you had left, I suspect you wouldn't have bought it anyway.

Besides, I have a feeling most readers would forget half of them before reaching the next page.

You're welcome.

And here it is: the entire Greek alphabet, all in one place.

This is probably the page you'll come back to most often, so try not to spill anything on it.

| β | Beta | B |
| γ | Gamma | G |
| δ | Delta | D |
| ε | Epsilon | E |

A few things to remember:

- Sigma has two lowercase forms: σ and ς.
- TH, PH, CH and PS each represent one Greek letter.
- Eta is Ē, while Epsilon is E.
- Omega is Ō, while Omicron is O.
- A rough breathing at the beginning of a word adds H. With initial Rho, write RH.

There. Twenty-four letters, one table, and considerably fewer excuses.
